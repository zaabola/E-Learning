# Tip cards: delivery (pace, fillers, eye contact, voice, energy, freeze recovery)

## pace-slow
strategy: slow_down
patterns: rushing, rushing_nervous, filler_burst
hint: Slow down a little
action: Say your next sentence at half speed
pause: Stop. Breathe, then restart slower than feels natural
why: Nerves speed us up, and fast talk is harder to follow.
source: SUSO

Speaking rate tends to rise with nervousness. Listeners need time to process ideas, especially new or technical
ones, so delivery chapters advise a rate slightly slower than conversation and deliberate variation: slower for
key points, a little faster for familiar material. What feels painfully slow to a nervous speaker usually sounds
normal to the audience.

## pause-key-point
strategy: silent_pause
patterns: rushing, rushing_nervous, filler_burst, monotone
hint: Pause after this point
action: After your next key idea, stop for two seconds
pause: Stop for three seconds. Then say your next point clearly
why: A silent pause lets the idea land and resets your pace.
source: SUSO

Pauses are a delivery tool, not a mistake. A short silence before or after an important idea signals that it
matters and gives listeners time to absorb it. Speakers who fear silence fill it with words or speed; using
planned pauses slows the overall rate and makes the speaker sound more in control.

## filler-silent
strategy: silent_pause
patterns: filler_burst
hint: Pause instead of "um"
action: When you feel an "um" coming, close your mouth and pause
pause: Stop. Silent 2 seconds, then the next sentence
why: Fillers fill thinking time; a silent pause does the same, cleaner.
source: Clark 2002

Research on spontaneous speech shows "uh" and "um" are used to signal that the speaker is planning and a delay
is coming. The planning time is needed; the sound is not. Delivery textbooks call these vocalised pauses and
suggest replacing them with a brief silence, which listeners hear as thoughtfulness rather than hesitation.

## filler-chunk
strategy: slow_down
patterns: filler_burst, rushing
hint: Shorter sentences
action: Finish this sentence, then start the next one fresh
pause: Pause. Say your next point in one short sentence
why: Long, unplanned sentences are where fillers pile up.
source: EPS

Fillers cluster where the speaker is building a sentence on the fly. Speaking in shorter, complete sentences and
knowing the next point in advance (from an outline rather than a script) gives fewer moments of searching for
words, so fewer "um" and "like".

## eye-anchor
strategy: eye_anchor
patterns: eye_contact_low, confidence_drop
hint: Look at the camera
action: Finish this sentence looking into the lens
pause: Pause. Look at the camera, then say your next point to it
why: Eye contact builds trust and makes you look confident.
source: SUSO

Eye contact is one of the strongest nonverbal signals of confidence and credibility. Speakers who read from notes
or look at the floor lose connection with listeners. The advice is to glance at notes only to pick up the next
idea, then deliver it while looking at the audience; on video, the audience is the camera lens.

## eye-notes
strategy: eye_anchor
patterns: eye_contact_low
hint: Glance, then look up
action: Read one keyword, then say the idea to the camera
pause: Pause. Find your next keyword, look up, then speak
why: Keyword notes let you look at the audience most of the time.
source: EPS

Speaking from full sentences on paper pulls the eyes down. Textbooks recommend speaking notes with keywords only:
look down, pick up the word, look up and say the idea in your own words. This keeps eye contact high without
memorising the talk.

## voice-emphasis
strategy: voice_vary
patterns: monotone, low_energy
hint: Stress one key word
action: In your next sentence, hit the most important word harder
pause: Pause. Say your next point like it is the best news today
why: Varied pitch and stress keep listeners following you.
source: SUSO

A monotone voice makes even good content hard to follow. Vocal variety, changing pitch, volume and rate, tells
listeners what matters. A simple way in is to pick the key word in each sentence and emphasise it, the way you
would in an animated conversation.

## energy-project
strategy: energy_up
patterns: low_energy, monotone, confidence_drop
hint: A bit louder
action: Speak to someone at the back of the room
pause: Pause, breathe in, and restart louder and brighter
why: Projection raises energy and sounds more confident.
source: EPS

Volume and energy should be a little higher than in conversation, because a talk has to carry. Speakers who lose
energy drop in volume first. Imagining a listener at the back of the room is a common cue to project without
shouting, and the extra breath support usually brings pitch variation back too.

## energy-why
strategy: reframe
patterns: low_energy, monotone
hint: Remember why it matters
action: Think of why this point matters to you, then say it
pause: Pause. Recall why you care about this, then continue
why: Enthusiasm in the voice follows from caring about the point.
source: EPS

Audiences mirror the speaker's energy. Textbooks note that enthusiasm cannot be faked for long, but it can be
found: connecting the current point to why it matters to you, or to the audience, brings back natural vocal
variety and energy.

## freeze-restart
strategy: restart_point
patterns: freeze
hint: Say your last point again
action: Repeat your last sentence, it will bring the next one
pause: Breathe. Look at your notes. Say: "So, the key point is..."
why: Restating the last idea is the fastest way back on track.
source: SUSO

Going blank happens to experienced speakers too. Common advice is not to apologise or panic: pause, breathe,
glance at your notes, and restate the last point or summarise where you are ("So far we have seen...").
Restating gives your memory a cue for what comes next, and the audience hears it as a summary, not a mistake.

## freeze-breathe
strategy: breathing
patterns: freeze
hint: Breathe, you have time
action: Breathe out slowly, then find your next keyword
pause: Stop. One long breath out. Then read your next keyword
why: A pause feels long to you, but short to the audience.
source: Bodie 2010

A blank mind under pressure is part of the arousal response: attention narrows onto the threat. Slowing the
breath and taking a moment to look at notes is better than filling the silence. A few seconds of silence feel
much longer to the speaker than to the listeners.
